Why

The council split 8-to-low looks like disagreement, but it isn't. The bull case and the bear cases agree on the same sentence: the appeal letter is not the product. Where they differ is what to do about it — the Expansionist wants to keep the letter as free bait and sell the outcome data behind it; the others want to widen the job around it. Neither is defending the PRD as written. Not one reviewer argued for shipping this as specified. The 8/10 is a score for a different, adjacent business.

The one place the council genuinely collides, the Researcher wins on evidence. The Expansionist's central claim is that mental-health parity law is an unproductized legal hammer and therefore the moat. It is a real hammer — and it is already built and given away. Counterforce Health ships a mental-health parity module that cross-references denials against parity precedent and drafts appeals citing clinical standards, free to patients and clinics, grant-funded. That is §3.3's "legal anchoring," shipped, at $0. The bull case survives only in its second half: the outcome ledger, which nobody has.

Where the reviewers converged

Three failure modes were reached independently by reviewers who never saw each other's work. Independent convergence is the strongest signal this exercise produces.

Contrarian · Buyer

No BAA means the first honest sales call ends the conversation

An EOB carries member ID, date of service, CPT and diagnosis — that is PHI, and a solo clinician is a covered entity. Uploading it to a vendor with no Business Associate Agreement is the therapist's violation, against their license and their malpractice carrier. The liability waiver in §3.4 does not cure this: HIPAA rights belong to the patient, who signed nothing. And the fix is expensive in exactly the wrong direction — HIPAA-eligible infrastructure plus a BAA chain that has to survive the leg where PHI reaches the model.

Logician · Buyer · Contrarian

A monthly subscription is the wrong shape for a twice-a-month event

A solo therapist bills roughly 85–100 claims a month. A meaningful share get denied — but most denials are corrections, not appeals: lapsed eligibility, wrong member ID, COB not updated, duplicate, missing modifier, deductible applied. Those are fixed by resubmitting a corrected claim or by billing the patient. The genuinely appeal-shaped subset is 1–3 a month, sometimes zero. "Unlimited appeals" is a promise that invites the customer to count, and the count is what cancels the subscription in month two.

All four bear-side reviewers

You automated the middle 20% and left both hard ends

The therapist doesn't learn about a denial from a mailed PDF — they learn from a $0 line item when the ERA posts, or from a client asking why they got a bill. That's upstream of your first screen. Downstream, a generic PDF is frequently the wrong artifact: payers want their own reconsideration form, through their own portal, to an address that varies by plan, inside a window that is 60 days for one payer and 180 for another. You hand over a download at the exact moment the real work begins.

The arithmetic

The Logician worked this from the bottom with no outside data, and the Buyer arrived at the same place from lived workflow. It is the cleanest reason the PRD fails as priced.

~90 claims/mo  →  denials  →  1–3 genuinely appealable (rest are resubmits)
1.5 appeals  ×  ~$100 recaptured  ×  ~40% win rate  =  ~$60/mo expected recovery
Price $99/mo against $60/mo recovered  =  ROI below 1.0
Effective cost ~$66 per appeal to chase a ~$100 claim
For $99 to clear a 3× buying bar → ~7–8 appealable denials/mo → ~450 claims/mo
= a 5–6 clinician group practice — which already pays a biller who does this and more

The price is aimed at a customer the product isn't built for, and the product is built for a customer the price doesn't fit. Separately, the 7-day trial with one appeal credit fully discharges the median customer's monthly need before any money changes hands — and an appeal takes 30–45 days to come back, so the trial window structurally cannot demonstrate that the letter works.

What the market already charges

The Researcher's finding is the one that most directly contradicts the PRD: nobody successfully charges providers a recurring subscription for this, and the two most visible competitors give the output away. Fight Paperwork removed its subscription wall entirely for providers. The only players charging money charge per case.

Risk and upside

Most likely to kill it

PHI without a BAA — a legal wall, not a growth problem

Every other objection here is fixable with pricing and scope. This one gates whether you may accept the first customer's document at all, it is the first question an audited therapist asks, and clearing it properly costs more per month than your first six subscribers pay — while the nearest substitute is free. It has to be answered before the funnel, not after.

Strongest reason to do it

Nobody owns behavioral-health appeal outcome data

The letter is a commodity — a general-purpose model writes it, and two funded competitors give it away. A payer-level ledger of what actually gets overturned — by denial code, payer, CPT, and argument used — is not a commodity, does not exist, and compounds with every appeal run through the system. It is what turns a price into performance pricing and what makes an EHR or billing network want to buy you instead of clone you. It is also the one asset the free competitors are not structurally motivated to build.

Money read

Price. $29–49 per appeal — which is, not coincidentally, exactly what the one competitor that successfully charges money charges. Per-event pricing flips the customer's math from 0.6× to roughly 3×, removes the recurring cancel decision, and turns churn from a failure into a non-event. If you want a subscription at all, the ceiling evidence puts a single-feature bolt-on at $15–29/mo, not $99. Hold three figures back for group practices and billing services, where the volume math already closes.

Time to first dollar. A $99/mo subscription sold cold to solo therapists inside 60 days is unlikely. A $29 one-off, bought in the moment of pain, is plausible. Note which of those two your deadline can actually survive.

Can you ship fast? Yes — and it doesn't help. An AI-assisted build makes this a days-long project, which means building was never the bottleneck. Therapists don't answer cold email, aren't on LinkedIn for vendors, and the communities where they gather ban vendor promotion on sight. You are about to spend a 30–60 day runway on the one part that was already cheap.

The denominator is shrinking underneath you. Roughly 38% of psychologists accept no insurance at all, and the therapists who do are migrating to Headway, Alma and Grow — platforms that absorb denials on the provider's behalf and monetize as a cut of reimbursement rather than a line item. "Solo therapist who bills insurance themselves" is the residual after that migration, self-selected for having chosen to keep 100% of reimbursement rather than pay a platform. That is the most price-sensitive cohort in the market, and you are asking it for the most money.

The cheapest 48-hour test

Write no product code. Sell the outcome by hand first. Two riskiest assumptions sit under the whole PRD — that the denials therapists actually receive are appealable at all, and that a therapist will hand money to a stranger to fix one. A concierge test answers both in the same motion, for $0, without touching a line of the stack.

  1. Offer twenty self-billing therapists a hand-written appeal for $40

    Find them where they already are — private-practice and therapist-billing groups, local licensure networks, group-practice owners. The offer is literal: send me your last denial, I'll write the appeal myself, $40. You are not pretending to have software. You are finding out if the job is worth money.

  2. Take the denial de-identified

    Ask for the payer, the CARC/RARC code, the CPT and the denial language — not the client's name or member ID. No PHI in your hands means no BAA required, which is the entire reason this test is legal to run tomorrow and the $99/mo product is not.

  3. Sort everything that arrives into four buckets

    Resubmit a corrected claim · bill the patient · genuinely appeal · call the payer. This sort costs an afternoon and tells you whether you are building an appeal-letter tool or a triage tool. Log every one from the first day — that log is the outcome ledger, and it is the actual asset.

  4. Read the two numbers that decide the product

    How many of the twenty paid the $40? And what share of what they sent was genuinely appealable rather than a resubmission? Everything in the PRD is downstream of those two figures.

Two kill conditions

If nobody pays $40 for you to write one by hand, the $99/mo product has no floor under it and no amount of UI fixes that. If under roughly 30% of what arrives is genuinely appealable, the PRD's central premise is wrong — build the thing that reads a denial and says which of the four actions is correct. That event happens weekly instead of quarterly, it has a determinable right answer, and appeal generation becomes one branch of it rather than the whole product.

If you reshape

Six changes. None require different technology, and none invalidate the work already in the PRD — §3.2's verification UI and §3.4's waiver survive intact, and read as compliance assets rather than friction once you sell to groups and billers.

  • Job → triage

    Lead with "what is this denial and what do I do about it," not "write me a letter." Roughly 4× the frequency, a verifiable right answer, and it makes the letter a feature instead of the whole product.

  • $99/mo → per appeal

    $29–49 an event. Match the pricing shape to the usage shape. Reintroduce a subscription only once triage gives it a weekly reason to exist.

  • PDF → submission packet

    Output the payer's actual required form, the correct portal or address, and the filing deadline. This is the part the customer said they'd pay $99 for.

  • PHI → de-identified

    Ship a paste-only MVP that never touches identifiers. It sidesteps the BAA wall entirely for validation and buys you months to build the real compliance chain — or to learn you don't need to.

  • Cold solos → the layer above

    Billing services and 5–30 clinician group practices. Ten relationships instead of a thousand customers, and they already hold BAAs. Pair it with long-tail pages on specific denial codes, where the search intent already exists at the moment of the wound.

  • Logs → outcome ledger

    §4's structured logging is listed as a post-launch nice-to-have. Move it to day one and make it the asset: denial code, payer, CPT, argument used, result, dollars recovered. Every month generating letters without capturing outcomes is compounding you never get back.
